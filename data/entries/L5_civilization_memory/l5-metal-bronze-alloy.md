# Bronze Alloys

## Overview

- Bronze: copper + tin (classical alloy)
- Brass: copper + zinc (common substitute)
- Bell metal: bronze + more tin (18-20%)
- Phosphor bronze: small phosphor addition
- All harder than pure copper alone
- All castable at lower temps than iron
- Bronze melts at 1800F vs iron at 2800F

## Copper-Tin Bronze

- Standard: 90% copper, 10% tin
- Good balance: strength and castability
- Higher tin (12-15%): harder, more brittle
- Lower tin (5-8%): softer, more ductile
- Tin is rare; use what is available
- Test alloys by casting and filing
- Springs: 8% tin; bearings: 10-12% tin

## Copper-Zinc Brass

- Standard: 70% copper, 30% zinc
- Easier to work than tin bronze
- Yellow color; distinctive appearance
- Zinc melts at 787F; adds to copper melt
- Zinc volatilizes above 1760F; work quickly
- Good for: fittings, springs, decorative
- Zinc fumes: toxic; ventilate workspace

## Bell Metal

- 78% copper, 22% tin proportion
- Produces clear ringing tone when struck
- Too hard to forge; cast only
- Polish: bright gold color when new
- Used for bells, gongs, cymbals
- Acoustic qualities require exact ratio
- Test bell quality by striking; listen

## Melting and Alloying

- Melt copper first in graphite crucible
- Add tin or zinc to molten copper
- Stir with clay-coated rod
- Skim dross off surface before pouring
- Pour at just above melting point
- Superheat briefly; no more than needed
- Brass: work fast; zinc escapes as vapor

## Bearing Metal

- 83% copper, 7% lead, 7% tin (babbitt)
- Lead improves lubricity and wear
- Pour as thin liner in bearing housing
- Excellent for axle and shaft bearings
- Reduces friction without steel balls
- Traditional millwork and wagon use

## Long-Term Value

- Bronze civilization preceded iron 2000 yr
- Copper and tin available globally
- Bronze tools adequate for many tasks
- Bronze age technology is highly capable
- Transition: iron bloomery when tin runs out
