# Metal Casting

## Overview

- Casting: pour liquid metal into mold
- Mold shape becomes final part shape
- Bronze: easier to cast than iron
- Gray iron: castable in simple cupola
- Sand casting: most versatile method
- Lost wax: for complex detail work
- Finish by filing and grinding after cast

## Pattern Making

- Pattern: full-size model of desired part
- Wood, clay, or wax patterns all work
- Add draft angle: 2-5 degrees taper
  allows pattern removal without damage
- Pattern slightly oversized: metal shrinks
  on cooling (1/4 in per foot for iron)
- Parting line: where mold halves meet
- Place parting line carefully for release

## Green Sand Mold

- Green sand: damp molding sand, no baking
- Mix: 85% sharp sand, 10% clay, 5% water
- Should clump when squeezed; not wet
- Flask: two-part wooden box for mold
- Pack sand around pattern firmly
- Add sprue (pour hole) and riser (vent)
- Remove pattern; close flask; pour

## Lost Wax Casting

- Carve or model in beeswax
- Invest (coat) with clay slip layers
- Dry thoroughly between layers
- Fire to melt and burn wax out
- Pour metal into empty cavity
- Break mold to release casting
- High detail; mold destroyed each use

## Bronze vs Iron

- Bronze melts at 1800F; iron at 2800F
- Bronze: simple clay furnace suffices
- Iron: needs bloomery or cupola
- Bronze: no post-work hardening
- Iron: can be hardened to superior edge
- Bronze alloys more forgiving for casting
- Start with bronze; advance to iron

## Finishing Castings

- Remove sprue and riser with hacksaw
  or chisel while still warm
- File or grind to final dimensions
- Internal surface rough; drill or ream
  holes to size
- Bronze: file to bright finish
- Iron: grind, then heat treat if needed

## Long-Term Value

- Casting enables bells, gears, weights,
  pulleys, fittings, pots, tools
- Multiplies blacksmith output dramatically
- Bronze casting: Bronze Age technology;
  2 people can operate a small foundry
- Community investment with lasting return
