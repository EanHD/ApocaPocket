# Timber Frame Joints

## Overview

- Mortise: rectangular pocket in timber
- Tenon: projecting tongue on beam end
- Together: mortise and tenon (M&T)
- Locked with hardwood pegs (trunnels)
- Drawbore: peg pulls joint tight
- Joints transfer shear, tension, compression
- Timber frame stands without any metal

## Tools Required

- Mortising chisel: 1 to 1.5 in wide
- Mallet: heavy wooden mallet
- Timber frame square for layout
- Slick (large paring chisel) for cleanup
- Brace and 1 in bit for peg holes
- Drawknife or spokeshave for pegs

## Mortise Layout

- Mark center line on timber face
- Mortise width: typically 1.5 in
- Mortise depth: 3 to 4 in
- Length: matches tenon shoulder width
- Bore series of holes inside layout lines
- Chop walls square with mortise chisel

## Tenon Layout

- Tenon thickness: same as mortise width
- Tenon length: 3 to 4 in (fits mortise)
- Shoulders: cut square around all sides
- Saw cheeks (long cuts) first
- Saw shoulders second
- Test fit; adjust with slick or rasp

## Drawbore Pegging

- Dry-fit joint completely
- Bore 1 in hole through post and tenon
- Disassemble; shift tenon hole 1/16 in
  toward shoulder (offset toward joint)
- Drive peg; draws joint tight as it aligns
- Peg wood: dried oak, ash, or locust
- Taper peg tip; drive with mallet

## Common Joint Types

- Simple M&T: post to beam, basic
- Housed M&T: beam shoulder fits housing
- Wedged M&T: pegs replaced by wedges
- Half-dovetail: resists pulling apart
- Tying joint: connects plates at corners
- Scarf joint: extends timber length

## Long-Term Value

- Metal-free joints last indefinitely
- Joints can be disassembled if needed
- Buildings can be relocated
- Skills require practice but are teachable
- Same joints used in furniture making
