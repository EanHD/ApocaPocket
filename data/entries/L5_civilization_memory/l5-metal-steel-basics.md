# Basic Steel Making

## Overview

- Steel: iron with 0.3-1.5% carbon by weight
- Carbon raises hardness potential
- Hardness enables cutting edges
- Too much carbon: brittle; cannot forge
- Cementation: add carbon to wrought iron
- Case hardening: add carbon to surface only
- Quench and temper: final heat treatment

## Carbon Sources

- Charcoal: primary carbon source
- Bone charcoal: adds carbon and case
- Wood ash: surface carbon addition
- High-carbon steel can come from recycled
  springs, files, rail steel if available
- Test unknown steel: spark test on grinder
  shows carbon content by spark pattern

## Cementation Process

- Pack wrought iron bar in charcoal powder
- Seal in clay pot or tube with clay
- Heat to cherry red (1600F) for many hours
- Carbon diffuses into iron slowly
- Time determines depth of carbon uptake
- 24-hour heat yields blister steel
- Fold and weld: produces shear steel

## Case Hardening

- Heat iron piece to cherry red
- Quench in bone char, carbon powder, or
  charcoal dust; surface absorbs carbon
- Reheat to orange; quench in water
- Only surface layer is hardened
- Interior remains soft and tough
- Good for: hammers, punches, dies

## Hardening and

- Heat steel to non-magnetic point
  (Curie point: 1418F; test with magnet)
- Remove magnet; heat until just above
  non-magnetic (cherry to bright orange)
- Plunge into quench: water, brine, or oil
- Water: fastest quench; risk of cracking
- Oil: slower; less cracking for complex tools
- Brine: between water and oil in speed

## Tempering

- After quench: steel is hard but brittle
- Polish bright; heat gently over flame
- Watch color change on polished surface:
- Pale straw: 430F; scrapers
- Straw: 450F; hand tools
- Blue: 570F; springs, chisels
- Grey: 620F; toughest; axes
- Quench at target color to arrest temper

## Long-Term Value

- Steel tools last 10-100x longer than iron
- All cutting tools require steel
- Enables saw blades, drill bits, files
- One blacksmith with steel skills
  transforms community tool capacity
