# Wrought Iron

## Overview

- Wrought iron: iron with slag fibers, <0.1% carbon
- Very low carbon: soft, ductile, weldable
- Bends without breaking unlike cast iron
- Welds to itself at welding heat
- Resists rusting better than mild steel
- Cannot be hardened by quench (too soft)
- Must case-harden or add carbon for tools

## From Bloom to Bar

- Bloom from smelt: spongy, full of slag
- Heat to orange-yellow: working heat
- Hammer all over to consolidate
- Fold and weld repeatedly: shingling
- Each fold expels slag and welds iron
- Result: coherent bar iron; fibrous inside
- Final draw: hammer into rod or bar shape

## Working Temperatures

- Black: 700F; do not work; risk cracking
- Red: 1400F; light work, finishing only
- Orange: 1800F; main forging range
- Yellow: 2100F; maximum safe work temp
- White/welding: 2400F; surface sparks
- Work in orange to yellow; check often
- Never let a heat drop to red while forging

## Basic Forging Ops

- Drawing out: lengthen, thin the bar
- Upsetting: thicken, shorten a section
- Bending: over hardy or horn of anvil
- Punching: hot-punch holes with drift
- Splitting: hardy cut on edge of anvil
- Welding: flux both surfaces; spark heat

## Forge Welding

- Both pieces at welding heat (white)
- Sand or borax flux prevents oxidation
- Strike immediately and decisively
- Two to three sharp blows to seat weld
- Work quickly; heat drops fast
- Check weld: cold chisel; no separation
- Multiple welds for long joints

## Slag Removal

- Visible slag inclusions: gray streaks
- They are a weakness; remove by welding
- More folds = cleaner iron = stronger
- Bloomery iron needs 5+ folds minimum
- Proper bar shows bright uniform interior
- Fibrous structure gives toughness

## Long-Term Value

- Self-sufficient iron production possible
- Wrought iron is foundational material
- Every nail, hinge, tool begins here
- Blacksmithing the highest-value craft
  in pre-industrial communities
- Pair with steel knowledge for hard tools
