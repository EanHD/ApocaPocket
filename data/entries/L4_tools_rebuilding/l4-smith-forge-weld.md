# Forge Welding

## What Forge Welding Is

- Heating two steel pieces to near-melting
- Hammering together while hot
- Steel fuses at grain level
- Stronger than the original steel
- Used before electric welding existed
- Requires precise heat control

## Flux Purpose

- Flux prevents oxidation at weld heat
- Oxide scale on steel prevents welding
- Common flux: borax (20 Mule Team)
- Apply to heated steel before final heat
- Borax melts to glass, seals surface
- Shields from oxygen in forge

## Heat Sequence

- Bring to bright orange, pull out
- Apply flux by sprinkling or dabbing
- Sizzles, melts onto steel surface
- Return to fire, bring to yellow-white
- Steel should appear to have a "wet" shine
- Small sparks beginning to fly: ready

## Weld Temperature Signs

- Yellow-white with wet, glistening look
- "Greasy" appearance when rotating
- Just before sparks stream from surface
- If steel sparks heavily: too hot (burning)
  Burning steel cannot be repaired
- No shine at all: too cool to weld

## Hammer Technique

- Work quickly from fire to anvil
- First blow: light tap to set pieces together
- Subsequent blows: firm and decisive
- Work from center outward
- Do not let cool spots go unworked
- Reheat if movement slows or color fades

## Types of Forge Welds

- Lap weld: overlap ends, hammer flat
- Scarf weld: taper both ends, overlap
- Jump weld: ends butted together
- V-joint: two pieces joined at angle
- Chain link: welding links together
- Faggot weld: fold piece onto itself

## Practice Project

- Weld a pair of tongs
- Draw out two bars, form jaw shapes
- Weld jaws onto square stock
- Forge pivot hole, rivet together
- First successful weld proves capability
- Start with mild steel, not high carbon
