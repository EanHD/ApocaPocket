# Steel Tempering

## Why Temper

- Freshly quenched steel is maximum hard
- Also maximum brittle: breaks like glass
- Tempering adds toughness by reducing hardness
- Trade some hardness for toughness
- Find balance for the tool's use
- Cutting tools: harder; impact tools: tougher

## Temper Color Chart

- Pale yellow (220C): razors, scrapers
- Straw yellow (240C): knife blades, drills
- Dark straw (255C): taps, die cutters
- Bronze (265C): axes, chisels for steel
- Purple-brown (280C): chisels for wood
- Bright blue (300C): springs, saws
- Dark blue (315C): toughest temper

## Polished Temper

- After quenching, sand to bright polish
- Hold polished section over heat source
- Watch edge carefully: colors creep in order
- Let straw yellow reach edge of cutting tool
- Quench when target color hits working edge
- Or let blue reach spine while straw at edge

## Heat Source Options

- Hot charcoal: difficult to control
- Hot metal block: even, predictable heat
- Open flame: works but watch closely
- Oil or salt bath: most even, professional
- Oven (household): consistent temperature
- Set oven to 220-300C for desired color

## Oven Tempering

- Harden multiple pieces, then oven temper
- Set oven to target temperature
- Place hardened and cleaned pieces on rack
- 1 hour at temperature (minimum)
- Allow to air cool in oven
- No quench needed for oven tempering

## Testing the Temper

- File test: file should skate off, not bite
  (too hard is fine, too soft fails)
- Impact test: strike corner with hammer
  Should dent, not chip (correct temper)
  Chips: too hard, temper hotter
  Deforms too much: too soft, re-harden
- Spring: should flex and return (blue temper)

## Re-tempering Old Tools

- Old tools often soft from unknown heat
- Re-harden then re-temper from scratch
- Test hardness with file first
  (saves time if already hard enough)
