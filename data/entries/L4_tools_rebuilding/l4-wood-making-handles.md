# Making Tool Handles

## Best Wood Species

- Ash: most common, flexible and tough
- Hickory: strongest, traditional American
- Osage orange: very hard, lasts longest
- Hornbeam: dense, hard, excellent
- Oak: adequate, but brittle vs ash
- Avoid: pine, poplar, most softwoods

## Selecting the Blank

- Straight grain is critical
- No knots in grip area
- Rive (split) blank from straight log
  for grain alignment with handle axis
- Sawn blank: grain may angle across
  Breaks under impact
- Rived blank: all grain runs with handle

## Roughing to Shape

- Start with blank 20% larger than finished
- Drawknife on shave horse: rough shape
- Work toward final profile progressively
- Round corners to octagonal first
- Octagonal to round with spoke shave
- Check with hand: should fit comfortably

## Handle Profiles

- Straight: hammers, hatchets
- Curved: axes, scythes (S-curve)
- Flared end: prevents slipping
- Eye end: should match tool eye exactly
- Taper eye end to fit from small to large
- Wedge locks handle in: crucial for safety

## Eye Fit and Wedge

- Eye end slightly small is correct
- Drive handle in from top with mallet
- Head weight drives it on as you work
- Cut kerf (1-2cm deep) at eye end
- Drive wooden wedge into kerf after fitting
- Add cross metal wedge perpendicular
- Test: swing tool; head must not slide

## Finishing

- Sand or scrape to smooth finish
- Avoid paint (hides cracks)
- Linseed oil: penetrates, toughens wood
- Apply 3-4 coats, allow to absorb
- Do not varnish (slippery when sweaty)
- Re-oil annually or when wood feels dry

## Spare Handle Storage

- Shape 2-3 handles per common tool type
- Store oil-treated in dry location
- Ready to fit immediately when needed
- Label by tool type: axe, sledge, hoe
- Replace any handle with visible cracks
