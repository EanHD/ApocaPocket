# Wood Splitting

## Riving vs Sawing

- Riving: splitting along grain with froe
- Sawing: cutting across or along grain
- Rived wood: stronger (follows grain)
- Sawn wood: may cut across grain = weak
- Riving is faster and needs no power
- Best for: shingles, tool handles, chair parts

## The Froe

- L-shaped blade with handle at 90 degrees
- Blade driven into wood, then levered
- Splits wood along grain predictably
- Make from flat steel bar with hole for handle
- Handle: any straight-grained wood
- Alternative: chisel and mallet for small splits

## Splitting Technique

- Work on fresh-cut green wood
- Place froe blade on end grain
- Strike back of blade with wooden mallet
- Rock handle to open split
- Guide split by angling handle
- Angling toward center = split steers center
- Angling to one side = split steers that way

## Understanding Grain

- Radial split (through center): best boards
- Tangential split: more warping
- Knots cause grain to spiral: splits deviate
- Straight-grained logs split perfectly
- Twisted grain: splits unpredictably
- Select straight-grained logs for riving

## What to Rive

- Shingles: 1-2cm thick from clear wood
- Boards for furniture and boxes
- Chair legs and rungs (turned on lathe)
- Tool handles: rived blanks for shaping
- Basket splints (very thin, flexible)
- Fence rails and stakes

## Cleaving Horse / Brake

- Simple sawhorse-style device
- Holds log while riving
- Foot pedal holds piece in place
- Frees both hands for froe work
- Build from 4 sticks and a fulcrum
- Traditional workshop basic equipment

## Species for Riving

- Oak: excellent, traditional shingles and timber
- Ash: ideal for handles, straight grain
- Chestnut: easy to split, rot resistant
- Cedar: shingles, very straight grain
- Avoid: elm, cherry (interlocked grain)
