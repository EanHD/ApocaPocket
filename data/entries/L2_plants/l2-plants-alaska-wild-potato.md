# Alaska Wild Potato

**ROOTS ARE SAFE. SEEDS ARE DEADLY.** This distinction cost Chris McCandless his life (August 1992, Stampede Trail).

## Two Species

**H. alpinum (Eskimo Potato)  -  roots SAFE:**
- Height: 6 - 16 inches, few stems
- Leaves: pinnate, 13 - 19 oval leaflets with blunt tips
- Flowers: pink to magenta, dense cluster, June - July
- Roots: white to pale yellow tubers, 2 - 4 inches, sweet/nutty smell
- Habitat: alpine tundra, dry riverbanks, well-drained soil

**H. mackenzii (Wild Sweet Pea)  -  seeds DEADLY:**
- Height: 12 - 24 inches, multiple branching stems
- Leaves: pinnate, 11 - 21 leaflets with narrow pointed tips
- Flowers: pinkish-purple, looser cluster, June - August
- Roots: deeper (6 - 12 inches)
- Habitat: similar to H. alpinum, slightly wetter soils

Differences are subtle  -  even experts can confuse these species. **Never eat seeds from either.**

## McCandless Case

- Ate what he believed were wild potato seeds (H. alpinum or possibly H. mackenzii)
- ODAP toxin accumulated over days: leg weakness -> lower limb paralysis -> unable to walk or forage
- Died of starvation compounded by neurotoxicity
- His field guide listed wild potato as edible but did not specify roots only
- Lesson: confirm species thoroughly before harvesting anything; roots only

## Positive ID

1. Location: tundra, alpine meadows, dry riverbanks; open sunny areas, well-drained soil
2. Structure: pea family; compound leaves with multiple leaflets arranged pinnately; no tendrils
3. Flowers June - July: pink to deep magenta; pea-like shape (banner, wings, keel); clustered raceme
4. Roots: dig 4 - 10 inches; look for white/yellow lateral tubers extending from main root; sweet smell when cut
5. Cross-verify all features; use laminated field guide with photos; when in doubt, do not harvest

## Safe Harvest

- Best time: spring April - June or fall September - October; avoid summer (roots depleted + both species flowering = highest confusion risk)
- Mark plants during flowering season; return in spring or fall for optimal roots
- Dig carefully 4 - 8 inches deep; extract lateral tubers; leave main root intact when possible
- Yield: 2 - 8 tubers per plant, each 2 - 4 inches long

## Preparation

- Raw: wash, slice thin; sweet and crunchy like mild parsnip
- Boiling 10 - 15 min: fork-soft, sweeter than potato
- Roasting 20 - 30 min: wrap in leaves, place in coals
- Short-term storage: cool dry bag 1 - 2 weeks; long-term: slice thin, sun-dry 2 - 3 days, sealed container 2 - 6 months
- Nutrition: 60 - 80 kcal/100g, 15 - 20g carbs; excellent wild carbohydrate source in starch-scarce Alaska

## ODAP Toxin

- Found in seeds of H. mackenzii; possibly also in H. alpinum seeds  -  assume ALL seeds are toxic
- Mechanism: mimics glutamate, over-excites neurons, causes lathyrism (neurotoxic paralysis)
- Progression: days 1 - 3 leg weakness -> days 3 - 7 lower limb paralysis, bladder dysfunction -> days 7 - 14 possible permanent paralysis, respiratory failure, death
- Recovery possible if exposure stops early with medical care; in wilderness recovery is unlikely

## Safety Checklist

Before harvesting:
- Identified H. alpinum by at least 4 features: height, leaf shape, flower cluster density, root color
- Ruled out H. mackenzii (taller, branching stems, narrower pointed leaflets)
- Harvesting in spring or fall, NOT summer
- Collecting ROOTS ONLY  -  no seed pods mixed into collection
- Plants were confirmed during a previous flowering season

## Range

- Throughout Alaska: Denali/interior, Matanuska Valley, Arctic tundra (North Slope)
- Also: Yukon Territory, northern British Columbia, Siberia
- Not found in coastal rainforests (too wet) or dense spruce forests (too shaded)
