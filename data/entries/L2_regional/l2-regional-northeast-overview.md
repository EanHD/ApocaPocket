# Northeast US Regional

Region: ME NH VT MA RI CT NY PA NJ MD DE WV. Hardiness zones 3b-7b.

## Forest Composition

Dominant biome: mixed deciduous and coniferous forest. Oak, maple, birch, beech, hemlock, white pine.
Foraging potential: among the highest in North America. Dense edible plant diversity in most areas.
Nut mast: white oak acorns (low tannin, edible after brief processing). Beech, hickory, black walnut.
Berries: blackberry, raspberry, blueberry, elderberry, serviceberry. Peak July through September.
Fungi: chicken of the woods, hen of the woods (maitake), chanterelle, oyster mushroom. Highly visible.
Spring greens: ramps, fiddlehead ferns, wood sorrel, garlic mustard (invasive, present everywhere).
Fall roots: Jerusalem artichoke, burdock root, cattail rhizome. High caloric value.
Toxic species: water hemlock (deadly), lily of the valley, false morel. Learn before eating any fungi.

## Watershed Density

Highest watershed density in the continental US. Running water within 0.5-3 miles in most terrain.
Surface water is abundant but requires treatment regardless of appearance or source.
Treatment: rolling boil for 1 minute, or 0.2-micron filter plus chemical treatment for Giardia and Cryptosporidium.
Spring seeps: common on hillsides and valley edges. Lower contamination risk than open streams.
Snow: safe source in winter if collected away from roads and animal trails. No treatment needed from clean source.
Avoid all water near former mills, factories, or railroad corridors. Heavy metals and PCBs persist for decades.

## Four Seasons Prep

Winter (Dec-Feb): blizzards, grid outages up to 2 weeks. Heat is the primary survival need.
Prep: backup heat source (wood stove or vented propane), 2-week food and water supply, snow shovel, ice cleats.

Spring (Mar-May): flooding, mud season. Tick season opens. Foraging season begins (ramps, fiddleheads).
Prep: waterproof boots, tick removal kit (fine-tip tweezers), monitor river gauges if near any waterway.

Summer (Jun-Aug): rare but deadly heat waves in cities. Prime foraging season. Tick and Lyme peak.
Prep: shade and hydration for heat. Full tick-check routine daily. Identify summer edibles in advance.

Fall (Sep-Nov): harvest and storage push. Prepare for first freeze. Nor'easter season opens in October.
Prep: maximize food storage. Winterize water pipes. Full storm kit ready by October 1.

## Urban vs Rural NE

Urban (Boston, NYC, Philadelphia, Baltimore):
- Dense population means fast resource competition after any significant disruption.
- Grid outage cuts water pressure in high-rises (pump-dependent). Store 1 gallon per person per day minimum.
- No foraging option. Food storage is the only buffer: 2 weeks minimum on hand.
- Evacuation: traffic gridlock within 2-6 hours of event. Leave before warning or shelter in place.
- Advantage: proximity to emergency services, hospitals, mutual aid networks, and organized relief.

Rural (Vermont, Maine, western PA and NY):
- Low population density means less competition and more available resources.
- Self-reliance is expected. Emergency services may take 1-4 hours or longer to respond.
- Foraging, hunting, fishing, and trapping are all viable food options year-round.
- Winter isolation: roads impassable 1-7 days after major ice or snow storm. Full self-sufficiency required.
- Advantage: space, clean water, firewood, game, and neighbors who are often experienced in self-reliance.
